How I teach AI

Lead with the problem. The tool comes last.

Most AI training starts with features. Mine starts with the work you're trying to get done. When you give AI the full context around a problem, the answers get better and you spend less time fixing them.

I've been at this longer than the current wave. I worked with Microsoft in 2010 training models to analyze unstructured data. I built custom automation for research workflows from 1984 to 1994, before off-the-shelf tools existed. Then I took a full year off to study how AI is taught and how people actually learn it, before I started teaching.

I don't promise 10x. I focus on better work and time you get back.

  1. GoalsWhat does done look like, and for whom?
  2. BlockersWhat's in the way right now?
  3. AudienceWho reads it, uses it, or approves it?
  4. IntentWhat should they think, feel, or do next?
  5. SkillsWhat do you bring, and what do you need AI to cover?
  6. Inputs and outputsWhat goes in, and what has to come out?
  7. Final deliverableThe format, length, and standard it has to meet.

Off the clock

Mountains and music.

I grew up in Chattanooga and spent my youth climbing, kayaking, caving, and camping in the mountains of Eastern Tennessee and North Georgia. That education never left me.

I've been a professional musician my whole life. These days music is also how I meet people. I often find fellow musicians in the other corners of my work, and we end up playing together.

Music isn't a hobby. It shapes how I think. The arc of an echo, the tension before a resolve.

My sister and I both have synesthesia. We hear colors and see sounds. It shows up in how I write, how I listen, and how I think through problems.
